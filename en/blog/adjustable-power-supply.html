12V DC Adjustable Power Supply — LM2596 with an External Potentiometer Mod

Ahmet Ulu · April 2026

Recently I wanted to build something that would be useful day to day and would also improve my electronics skills. I designed an adjustable DC power supply based on the LM2596. Standard LM2596 modules have a tiny trimpot on them — it is hard to use because it's so small, and fine adjustment needs a screwdriver. So I decided to remove this trimpot and replace it with an externally adjustable 10-turn precision potentiometer.

How the circuit is built

I used a 12V DC adapter as the input. Power from the adapter first goes to the DC input jack, then through a fuse and the on/off switch. After the switch, the line feeds the input of the LM2596 module. On the output side, I added positive and negative terminals and a digital voltmeter display to see the voltage in real time.

Technical Details

Input Voltage12V DC adapter
Output Voltage1.25V – 37V (adjustable)
Buck ConverterLM2596 adjustable module
Potentiometer10K 10-turn precision pot
Fuse2A
Display3-wire digital voltmeter
EnclosureA recycled strained-yogurt tub

Problems and how I solved them

The most educational part was the problems I ran into. First, because my solder wire was poor quality, I applied too much heat to the potentiometer and ruined it. I bought a new potentiometer and used better solder. But the circuit still didn't work.

The problem was the pin connections. Even though the numbering looks the same on every potentiometer, the physical order of the wiper and the outer pins can differ between models. I measured with a multimeter to find the real pinout.

There was one more issue: while soldering I had damaged the board's GND pad. So I took the GND connection directly from the negative output terminal instead of the board — in buck converter circuits the output negative and system GND are common. After making this connection, the system started working perfectly.

Testing

Once the system was working, I verified it with a multimeter. The device's own display and the multimeter showed the same value — calibration was spot on.

The enclosure: in the spirit of recycling

I reused an old strained-yogurt tub I had lying around as the enclosure. This hero, which normally served in the kitchen, has changed careers and now works in power electronics on my desk.

Conclusion

This project taught me how much solder quality matters, that too much heat can damage components, that you should measure instead of blindly trusting pin numbers, and how critical reading the datasheet is. Sometimes the fix isn't buying a new part — it's understanding the logic of the circuit.

In the future I'm thinking of adding a current meter, fan cooling and a USB output. For me, this wasn't just a circuit; it was real problem-solving practice.
