EMI in Class D Amplifiers and an LC Output Filter: My MAX98357A PCB Design

Ahmet Ulu · October 2026 · 4 min read
Contents

  2. The fix: a differential LC filter
  3. The maths: cut-off frequency
  4. What I did on the PCB for EMI
  5. Next step
  6. References

Class D audio amplifiers are very efficient, but that efficiency has a price: the output carries high-frequency switching components along with the audio. In this project I looked into that problem and designed a 2-layer PCB in KiCad with the MAX98357A I²S Class D amplifier IC and a differential LC output filter.

Class D amplifier board

The problem: switching noise and long cables

A Class D amplifier switches its output transistors on and off at a frequency far above the audio band; the audio is carried in the width of those pulses. The speaker does not reproduce the high frequencies anyway, but they keep travelling along the speaker cables. The longer the cable, the more it behaves like an antenna and radiates that energy, which can cause electromagnetic interference (EMI) in nearby electronics.

The MAX98357A is designed to run filterless with short cables, thanks to its edge-rate control. What I wanted to find out was how much difference an output filter makes once the cable gets longer. So I designed the board with a filter and put measurement points on both sides of it.

The fix: a differential LC filter

The MAX98357A output is BTL (bridge-tied load): the speaker sits between two outputs and both of them switch. So I built the filter symmetrically too: a 22 µH inductor in each output line and a 220 nF capacitor between the two filtered outputs.

The maths: cut-off frequency

For the differential signal the current flows through both inductors, so the capacitor works against a total of 2 × 22 = 44 µH:

f₀ = 1 / (2π · √(Ltotal · C))f₀ = 1 / (2π · √(44 µH · 220 nF)) ≈ 51.2 kHz

That is above the 20 kHz audio band and well below the switching frequency. Being a second-order filter, attenuation above f₀ grows by 40 dB per decade: in the ideal calculation the signal at 500 kHz is about -40 dB, roughly 1/100 in voltage.

One thing stood out in the plot: the damping of the filter depends on the speaker impedance. With an 8 Ω speaker Q ≈ 0.57 and the drop at 20 kHz is about 0.8 dB; with a 4 Ω speaker Q ≈ 0.28 and the drop at 20 kHz is about 4.2 dB. A real speaker's impedance changes with frequency, so checking this in LTspice and on the bench is my next step.

What I did on the PCB for EMI

  • Short high-current paths: I placed the inductors close to the IC outputs; keeping the switched-current loop small reduces radiated noise.
  • GND planes and stitching vias: both layers carry a ground pour, tied together with many vias to give return currents a short, low-impedance path.
  • Decoupling: the 100 nF and 10 µF capacitors sit right next to the IC supply pin.
  • Symmetry: I routed OUT_P and OUT_N and the two inductors as similarly as possible.
  • Measurability: test points for the outputs before (TP4, TP5) and after (TP6, TP7) the filter, 5 V (TP1), GND (TP2) and SD_MODE (TP3).

Instead of using a ready-made amplifier module, I put the IC directly on the board, so I could choose every detail of the filter and the layout myself.

Technical details

ICMAX98357A · I²S Class D, 5 V up to 3.2 W into 4 Ω
Output filter2 × 22 µH + 220 nF · f₀ ≈ 51.2 kHz
ConnectorsJ2: 7-pin I²S + SD_CTRL · J1: 5 V terminal · J3: speaker terminal
PCB2 layers, GND planes, 4 × M2 mounting holes
ToolKiCad

Next step

The design stage is done. Next I will simulate the filter in LTspice with different speaker loads, then build the board and measure the signals before and after the filter on an oscilloscope. I will compare the filtered and unfiltered cases with different cable lengths and share the results here.
