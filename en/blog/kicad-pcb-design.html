PCB Design with KiCad: Boards I Designed for Clients on Bionluk

This summer, the electronics work I spent the most time on was designing PCBs for clients through Bionluk, a Turkish freelance marketplace. I opened a listing for custom PCB design and have completed 8 orders so far, and every review I've received is 5 stars. I did every design in KiCad and handled the whole process myself, from the idea the client described to the schematic, the board layout and the production-ready files.

In this post I take a closer look at two designs without sharing any client details. Use the tabs above each design to switch between the schematic, PCB and 3D views, and tap any image to enlarge it.

How an order works

1. Understanding the need. Everything starts with listening to the client's project: what will the board do, which inputs and outputs are needed, how will it be powered, and are there any size or connector constraints? I don't start drawing until this part is clear.

2. Schematic. I build the schematic hierarchically: power input, microcontroller, output stage and input stage each live on their own sheet. This makes the design easier to read, both for me and for the client.

3. Placement and routing. I group and place components by function, route power traces wider than signal traces, and put the screw terminals on the edges where wiring is easiest.

4. Revision and delivery. I go through the design with the client, make the requested changes and deliver the board with production-ready files.

Design 1: Traffic Signal Control Board

A NodeMCU-based control board that drives traffic lights and reads button or sensor inputs. I built the schematic as a seven-sheet hierarchy: power input, NodeMCU control, output stage, input stage, expansion and connectors.

Traffic Signal Board

Outputs: To drive the lights I daisy-chained two 74HC595 shift registers. This gives 16 outputs while using only three NodeMCU pins (data, clock and latch).

Inputs: For buttons and sensors I used a PCF8574AP I²C port expander. Eight inputs can be read over just the SDA and SCL lines; the 4.7k pull-up resistors on those lines are in the schematic too.

Power and testing: The board is powered from a DC jack. It has a 1N5819 Schottky diode for reverse-polarity protection, 470µF and 100nF filter capacitors at the input and a power LED. I added a total of 24 test points across the inputs and outputs, which makes checking each signal with a multimeter much easier once the board is built.

Expansion: A 2×4 connector carries the continuation of the output chain, the I²C bus and power, so expansion boards with the same structure can be added. The board in the PCB and 3D tabs is that expansion board: it has a 74HC595, a PCF8574AP and screw terminals for light outputs and sensor inputs. I chose DIP packages for the ICs so they can sit in sockets and be swapped easily.

Design 2: Peltier Temperature Control Board

An ESP32-WROOM-32-based power and control board that drives six Peltier modules with PWM. The same board also manages fans, pumps and sensors. The schematic has nine sheets; here I show the control and driver sheets.

Peltier Control Board

Control: The ESP32 handles 6 Peltier PWM channels, 3 fans with tachometer inputs, 2 pumps, a temperature sensor, flow and level sensor inputs, and a UART line for an LCD. I also added a programming header along with reset and boot buttons, so firmware can be flashed easily while the board is in place.

Driver stage: The ESP32's 3.3V outputs aren't enough to fully turn on a power MOSFET switching a high-current load like a Peltier module. That's why I put TC4427 MOSFET drivers in between: each drives two channels, so three of them cover all six. Switching is done on the 12V rail with IRF2804 N-channel MOSFETs, and every Peltier output has a Schottky diode.
